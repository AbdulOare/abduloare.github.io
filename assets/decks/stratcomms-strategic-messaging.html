Part One · What Makes Messaging Strategic

A message is a decision.
Not a caption.

Communication is strategy made audible. If the message was not decided,
it will be improvised, and improvisation is where institutions bleed.
Part One · Tactical vs Strategic
06 / 29

Outputs are activity.
Outcomes are the mission.

Tactical communication
Strategic communication
Asks
“What do we say?”
“What must change in the hearer?”
Starts from
The event: something happened, respond to it.
The objective: something must be achieved, message toward it.
Measures
Outputs: releases issued, posts published, interviews granted.
Outcomes: what the audience now believes, feels, or does.
Time horizon
Today's headline.
The institution's position a year from now.
A press release is an output. A community that now cooperates is an outcome. Only one of them is the mission.

Part One · Case
09 / 29
A composite drawn from real Nigerian statements

Same failure. Same afternoon.
Opposite outcomes.

A federal agency's payment platform fails on salary day. Two ways to say so.
Statement A · As issued
“The Agency wishes to state that intermittent service disruptions may have affected some transactions. Stakeholders are enjoined to remain calm as modalities are being put in place.”
Hear it read · 15s
00:00 / 00:00
31 words · no owner · no date
What the public heard
“We will not tell you what happened, who owns it, or when it ends.” Trust spent, and the vacuum filled by rumour within the hour.
Statement B · The alternative
“Salary payments failed today. That is our failure. Payments resume within 48 hours and every affected worker will be paid in full. Next update: noon tomorrow.”
Hear it read · 13s
00:00 / 00:00
26 words · an owner · two dates
What the public heard
“They know. They own it. There is a date.” Trust earned, from the same bad news, because the message was decided, not drafted in fear.
The difference was never the incident. It was the decision about what the message had to do.
Part Two · Clarity · The First Discipline
10 / 29
Click each quadrant

Four ways an
institutional message
dies.

Every unclear message dies one of four deaths. Name the enemy in your own draft and you can defend against it.
01 · JARGON
Language nobody repeats
“Synergistic stakeholder engagement” says nothing and hides who acts. If a market trader cannot repeat it, redraft it.
02 · HEDGING
Reads as guilt
“It may be recalled that efforts are ongoing…” A hedged message sounds like concealment. Say what you know, and when you will say more.
03 · STUFFING
Seven points, zero retained
Every extra message taxes the one that matters. The audience keeps three at most. You choose which three, or chance does.
04 · AMBIGUITY
The vague line becomes the headline
Under pressure, your least precise sentence is the one that gets quoted. Precision is not pedantry. It is armour.
Part Two · The Clarity Test
11 / 29
Two questions · Ask them before anything goes out

The clarity test.

Test 1 · The hostile reader
Could a hostile editor misquote this?
Read your draft as your worst reader will, hunting for the half-sentence that stands alone as a damaging headline. If it can be misquoted, it will be.
Test 2 · The retelling
Could your most junior officer repeat it accurately?
A message travels by retelling: at the gate, in the queue, on WhatsApp. A message that cannot survive one retelling was never clear.
Clarity is not simplification. It is discipline about what you are willing to be quoted on.
Part Two · The Message House
12 / 29
Instrument 1 of 2 · You build one at 11:19

The Message House.

One structure that keeps every spokesperson, every channel, every day: on the same message.
The roof · Core message
One sentence. 27 words or fewer. The single idea every audience must hold.
Pillar 1
Supporting message: answers “why should I believe the roof?” for a named audience.
Pillar 2
Supporting message: a different angle, a different audience. Three pillars, never seven.
Pillar 3
Supporting message: often the one that answers the hostile audience before it asks.
The foundation · Proof points
Facts, figures, dates, precedents: verifiable and ready. Every pillar stands on at least one proof point, or it is an opinion in uniform.
If a pillar does not hold up the roof, it is furniture. Remove it.
Part Two · The 27-9-3 Rule
13 / 29
Instrument 2 of 2 · The discipline of length

27 words. 9 seconds. 3 messages.

27
Words in your core message
Long enough for a complete thought with an owner and a date. Short enough that nothing can be cut out of context.
9
Seconds spoken aloud
The length of a broadcast soundbite. Take longer and the editor cuts you: at a place you did not choose.
3
Messages per encounter
One interview, one townhall, one statement: three messages, never more. The audience keeps three. You choose which three.
The rule is not a cage. It is a filter. What survives 27 words is what you actually meant. Ukraine's president proved it in seven, in 2022: “I need ammunition, not a ride.”
Part Two · Before & After
14 / 29
One rewrite · Nothing true was lost

The same announcement, twice.

Before · As drafted
43 words · ~16 seconds
“The general public is hereby informed that the ongoing comprehensive upgrade of the recruitment portal may occasion temporary service interruptions, and applicants are enjoined to exercise patience as all necessary modalities have been put in place to ensure seamless service delivery.”
After · The 27-word discipline
20 words · ~7 seconds
“The recruitment portal closes Friday to Sunday for an upgrade. It reopens 8 a.m. Monday. Applications already submitted are not affected.”
The rewrite added a date, an instruction and a reassurance, and removed only the fog. “Exercise patience” is not a message.
Micro-Activity · The Rewrite
15 / 29
Solo or pairs · 5 minutes · Two share-outs

27 words or fewer. Go.

Activity Timer 05:00
Rewrite this · 52 words of fog
“The Corporation wishes to reassure its esteemed consumers that the observed disruption in water supply across some parts of the metropolis is receiving urgent attention at the highest levels, and that relevant technical teams have been fully mobilised to ensure the restoration of normal supply within the shortest possible timeframe, barring unforeseen circumstances.”
How to run it
  1. 3 minutes · rewrite in 27 words or fewer.
  2. Missing facts? Invent a date and an owner: mark them as placeholders.
  3. 2 share-outs: read aloud against the 9-second clock.
  4. Room applies the clarity test to each.
The standard
What happened, who owns it, when it ends, what to do meanwhile. An imperfect 27 words beats a perfect 40.
Part Three · Effectiveness · Audience & Persuasion
16 / 29
Click each quadrant

One message.
Four audiences.
Always.

You write for one audience. You are heard by four. The commonest failure is writing for the first, and being ambushed by the third.
01 · PRIMARY
Must act
The audience the message exists to move. They need instruction: what changes, from when, what to do. Write for them first.
02 · SECONDARY
Hears it anyway
Partners, press, the general public. They need information and context, and they judge your tone as much as your facts.
03 · HOSTILE
Reads it for the weakness
Critics and opponents hunting the misquotable line. They need deterrence: answer the accusation before it is asked.
04 · INTERNAL
Your own people
Staff hear every external message too. They need reassurance and the why, and they must never learn institutional news from the newspaper.
Part Three · Persuasion Fundamentals
17 / 29
Click each card to reveal

Aristotle still runs your press desk.

01 · WHO SAYS IT
Ethos
Credibility: the scarcest resource an institution has. Spent in one bad statement, rebuilt over years. Choose the messenger as deliberately as the message.
02 · WHAT IT MAKES THEM FEEL
Pathos
Emotion, disciplined. Name real fears honestly (safety, jobs, fairness) without exploiting them. An institutional voice that feels nothing persuades no one.
03 · WHAT PROVES IT
Logos
Evidence: dates, numbers, precedents, the foundation of your Message House. Logos is what your message stands on when the speaker leaves the room.
The modern institutional formula: lead with ethos, discipline the pathos, anchor in logos.
Part Three · Framing
18 / 29
One fact · Three honest frames

Framing is a choice.
Make it on purpose.

The fact
The Service recruited 3,000 new personnel this quarter. Its largest intake in a decade.
Frame 1 · Service
“Shorter queues, faster response.”
3,000 more officers serving the public. Must survive: “Will service actually improve, or just headcount?”
Frame 2 · Security
“3,000 more protecting communities.”
Presence where it was thin. Must survive: “Deployed where, and why not before?”
Frame 3 · Reform
“Merit-based, transparent intake.”
A recruitment the institution can defend list by list. Must survive: “Publish the list, then.”
The test is survival: a frame that collapses under one hostile question was not framing. It was wishful thinking.
Part Three · Channel–Message Fit
19 / 29

The channel is chosen last.
But it is still chosen.

Built for
The discipline
Press release
The record: accountability, quotable fact.
Every sentence must survive being quoted alone. No sentence you cannot defend in six months.
The post
Speed and reach, owning the first hour.
One message, plain words, no thread of ten. If it needs a thread, it needed a statement.
Townhall answer
Trust: in the room, face to face.
Answer the question actually asked, then bridge to your message. Evasion in person costs double.
Internal signal
Cohesion. Your people, aligned.
Specific and actionable, and it moves first, before any external release. Always.
The core message never changes across channels. The discipline does. That is fit.
Part Three · Case
20 / 29
One roof · Four doors

One incident, four audiences.

The situation · Core message
A popular over-the-counter service is withdrawn nationwide for security reasons, effective Monday.
Core message, 24 words: “From Monday, all applications move online to stop a document-fraud scheme. Pending applications remain valid. Help desks open in every state office from Friday.”
Internal · Moves first
Staff briefing, Thursday: the why, the fraud figures, the script for the counter. Your people are the first channel the public meets.
Primary · Applicants
Instruction: what changes, from when, what to do. SMS, radio and the portal banner. No word about the institution's feelings.
Secondary · The press
The record: release with fraud numbers, the date, the help-desk plan, and a named spokesperson taking calls.
Hostile · The critic
Pre-empt: “Yes, queues moved online; here is the offline help desk in all 36 states.” The accusation, answered before it is asked.
Four deliveries. One message. Internal before external: always.

Appendix A1 · Message House Reference
26 / 29

The Message House: template and worked example

The template · One flipchart sheet
ROOF: the core message. One sentence, ≤ 27 words, spoken in 9 seconds. Contains an owner and a date wherever possible.
PILLAR 1 / 2 / 3: supporting messages, each answering “why believe the roof?” for a named audience (primary · secondary · hostile · internal).
FOUNDATION: proof points. Verifiable facts, figures, dates and precedents. At least one per pillar.
TESTS: misquote test, retelling test, 27-word count, objective check. Run by someone who did not write the draft.
Worked example · Biometric verification scenario
Roof (24 words): “From June, biometric verification protects your record from fraud. Enrolment is free, takes ten minutes, and mobile teams reach every local government by May.”
Pillar 1 · Primary: Enrolment is free and fast: ten minutes, 774 centres plus mobile teams. Proof: centre list, published rollout calendar.
Pillar 2 · Hostile: No new fee: verification replaces charges paid to touts today. Proof: gazetted fee schedule; fraud-loss figures.
Pillar 3 · Secondary/Internal: Data is held under the NDPA 2023, audited annually. Proof: NDPC registration, audit commitment, named data officer.
Appendix A2 · Workshop Scenarios
27 / 29

Alternate scenario briefs & facilitation tips

Alternate briefs · One per table for a mixed pitch round
Scenario B · Sensitive institutional change
Your institution consolidates six zonal offices into three. No job losses, but 400 staff relocate, and two host communities lose their largest employer. A senator has already called it “abandonment.” Announce it.
Scenario C · Responding to a viral allegation
A video claims your agency's relief materials are being sold in an open market. The bags in the video carry your logo; the footage is eight months old, from a case you prosecuted. It has 2 million views by morning. Respond.
Scenario D · The good news nobody believes
Your service's processing time genuinely dropped from six weeks to five days. Public reaction to the announcement draft: “lies.” Build the house that makes good news credible.
Facilitation tips
Roof first, always. Tables that start with pillars build seven of them. The roof decides what qualifies as a pillar: stop and redirect any table debating pillars at minute four.
Force the audience tag. A pillar tagged “everyone” is tagged no one. Ask: “Which of the four hearers is this pillar for?”
Time the pitches ruthlessly. The 30-second clock is the lesson: a roof that cannot be delivered in 30 seconds is not a roof yet.
Appendix A3 · Glossary
28 / 29

Shared vocabulary: for all three days

Strategic communication: messaging that starts from an objective and is measured by outcomes: what the audience now believes or does.
Message: the working unit. One idea, one audience, 27 words or fewer, built and repeatable.
Narrative: the standing account connecting messages over time, from where the institution came to where it is going.
Story: one person, one moment, told concretely; the vehicle that makes narrative and message believable.
Alignment chain: values → objectives → narrative → messages → channels; every message traceable upward.
Message House: one roof message, three pillars, proof-point foundation; the session's central instrument.
27-9-3 rule: 27 words, 9 seconds, 3 messages per encounter; the discipline of length.
Clarity test: two questions. Could a hostile editor misquote it? Could a junior officer repeat it after one hearing?
Framing: deliberate choice among true emphases of the same fact; tested by survival under one hostile question.
Ethos / Pathos / Logos: credibility, disciplined emotion, evidence; lead with ethos, anchor in logos.
Audience map: primary (must act), secondary (hears anyway), hostile (reads for weakness), internal (your own people, told first).
Channel–message fit: the channel chosen last and matched to purpose. Record, speed, trust, cohesion, each with its discipline.
Proof point: a verifiable fact, figure, date or precedent that holds a pillar up; without one, a pillar is opinion.
Holding statement: a first, fast message that owns the facts you have and commits to the next update. Yemi's session builds on it.
